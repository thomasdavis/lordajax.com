# The $50k Job at the Top of My $150k List

_A week spent teaching a job matcher, a translator, and a canvas element to stop lying to me._

I built a job matcher for jsonresume that lets you set a minimum salary. I asked it for jobs paying at least $150k. It handed me, at the number one spot, a legacy .NET WebForms gig in a "min $150k" list that actually paid about $50k a year. Number four wanted me for a $300 total contract. You only find this by refusing to trust the code and scoring it instead, which is what most of the week became: writing evaluations that catch software being confidently wrong, then fixing what they caught.

## The job matcher was ranking on vibes

The jsonresume jobs API takes your resume, embeds it, and finds matching job postings. It worked in the demo-day sense — it returned jobs, they were sort of relevant, nobody looked too hard. Then I hand-rated a corpus of 100 real postings against my own profile: twelve "gold-best" jobs a good recruiter would surface, eight "gold-worst" ones that should never rank high. That frozen, hand-graded list is the whole trick — once you have a gold set, ranking quality stops being an opinion and becomes a number you can regression-test.

The numbers were bleak. The baseline was putting two of the eight known-bad jobs into the top ten, and precision-at-five — the fraction of the top five that were actually good — sat around 0.2. One in five.

Digging into why produced a useful list of failure modes, most of them the sort of thing that hides in any retrieval system until you measure it:

**Freshness was eating relevance alive.** The scoring blended cosine similarity with a recency bonus: `0.85 * similarity + 0.15 * recencyBoost`, the boost decaying with a 14-day half-life. Sounds reasonable — until you notice all the resume-vs-job similarities clustered in a narrow band between roughly 0.37 and 0.44. After the 0.85 weighting, the whole relevance signal could only move a result by about 0.06, while the recency term swung a full 0.15 from "posted today" to "posted three months ago." Freshness was worth more than twice whether the job actually matched you — which is how a stale .NET job posted yesterday beats a perfect match posted last week. The fix was almost embarrassing: rebalance to `0.95 * similarity + 0.05 * recencyBoost`, so recency is a tiebreaker inside the band instead of the headline act.

**The candidate pool was capped on the wrong score.** The database fetched the top-N candidates by *raw* similarity, then applied time-decay afterward. So a fresh job whose raw similarity ranked 101st could, after decay, deserve to be near the top — but it never entered the pool to be considered. The boundary was drawn with one ruler and everyone re-measured with a different one. Widen the pool before you decay.

**Two filters were no-ops that failed open.** The salary filter skipped any job whose `salary_usd` was null — and the ingest parser never populated that column, so it was null on all 100 jobs. Every job passed the "minimum salary" gate because the gate had nothing to check. The keyword filter, meanwhile, was doing `JSON.stringify(job).toLowerCase().includes("ai")`, which matched 98 of 100 jobs because "ai" is a substring of "maintain," "available," and "training." Searching for AI jobs returned a .NET job matched on the letters in *maintain*.

The rebuild that followed is a small IR curriculum, so here are the two ideas worth stealing.

**Hybrid retrieval.** A dense embedding is great at meaning and terrible at exact terms. It compresses "Rust," "Django," and specific company names into that same narrow similarity band, because semantically they're all just "software words." So the new matcher runs a second retrieval arm over Postgres full-text search — a `tsvector` index queried with `websearch_to_tsquery`, which understands quoted phrases and OR-ed terms the way a search box does — and fuses the two candidate lists. The vector arm finds jobs that *mean* what your resume means; the lexical arm rescues the job that literally says "Rust" when you literally wrote "Rust." Neither alone is enough; together they cover each other's blind spots.

**Rocchio relevance feedback.** When you mark jobs "not interested," the matcher should learn. Rocchio is the classic vector-space way to do this, from 1971 and still correct: you move the query vector toward the things the user liked and away from the things they rejected. The full formula nudges by both a positive and a negative centroid; here I only wired the negative arm. After you reject at least two jobs, it averages the embeddings of the rejected postings into a single "this is what you hate" direction, subtracts a fraction of that direction from your query embedding, and renormalizes:

```
export function subtractDirection(vec, negVec, weight = 0.15) {
  const adjusted = vec.map((v, i) => v - weight * negVec[i]);
  return normalize(adjusted);
}
```

That's `apps/registry/app/api/v1/jobs/matching/vectorMath.js`. It's three lines and it's the entire mechanism by which the search bends around your taste. The weight is deliberately small (0.12 live) — you want to lean away from rejected clusters, not teleport across the embedding space when someone dismisses one bad gig.

The part I'm proudest of isn't a feature, it's the guardrail. The eval now lives in the repo as deterministic tests with frozen fixtures, scored on three metrics. **NDCG@k** (normalized discounted cumulative gain) sums the relevance of each result but discounts it by `log2(position + 1)`, so a good hit at rank one counts fully and the same hit at rank ten counts a fraction — then divides by the perfect ordering's score so it lands between 0 and 1. **Recall@k** is the fraction of your known-good set that made the top k. And **worst-leakage** just counts how many known-bad jobs snuck into the top k — sometimes the most honest metric is "how much garbage is above the fold." CI runs these against the gold set on every change and fails the build if the ranking regresses: the same discipline as a snapshot test, pointed at search quality instead of rendered HTML. I haven't re-run the full live evaluation against production yet — the frozen fixtures are the before-picture and the ratchet, not a claim that the live ranking is now perfect.

## Teaching a one-way translation model to go backwards

mobtranslate's homepage could translate English into Kuku Yalanji but not back. The obvious move — run the model in reverse — doesn't work, because the fine-tuned model is one-directional: its own `/v1/model` endpoint literally reports "English to Kuku Yalanji." You can't flip a model that was only ever trained one way.

So reverse translation is a different animal: dictionary-evidence retrieval plus a single structured model pass. The interesting part is the retrieval, because Kuku Yalanji is agglutinative. In agglutinative languages a single surface word is a stem with endings glued on — "ngaykuwunbu" is the headword "ngayku" (my) carrying a suffix. Match whole tokens against dictionary headwords and you miss nearly every inflected word, because almost nothing appears in its bare dictionary form in a real sentence. So the scorer rewards a headword that is a *prefix* of the input token, scaled by how much of the token it covers — general string scoring over the dictionary, not a hand-maintained affix list you'd have to babysit forever and still get wrong for the next language.

Then there was a subtle handoff bug that only showed up in live testing. For "wanju ngaykuwunbu bayanba," retrieval correctly surfaced the entries for "ngayku" and "bayan" — but the model, reading the evidence, declared both source words "not in the dictionary" because it only accepted exact headword matches, and returned "Who ngaykuwunbu bayanba" at low confidence. The retriever already *knew* it was a stem match; it just wasn't telling the model that. The fix was to make each piece of evidence carry not only which dictionary entry matched but *how* it matched — exact, stem, clipped, or variant — and to state in the contract that a stem match means the headword genuinely is the base of the longer word, so the word is present even though its ending stays unverified. Same phrase now comes back as "who my camp" at high confidence, with the honest note that the endings on those two words couldn't be checked. That honesty caveat is the right posture for a low-resource language: say what you know, flag what you're guessing, don't paper over the morphology you couldn't verify.

The other mobtranslate chore: the Kuku Yalanji model runs on a hosted inference Space that returns a `503 model_loading` when it's idle and gone cold. Since it sits directly on a user's translate request, the first person to show up after a quiet spell got "translation unavailable." The cure is a keep-warm timer that pings the Space every fifteen minutes and self-skips when the model's disabled — a heartbeat so it never fully falls asleep on the critical path.

## A dithered canvas that couldn't agree with itself

tpmjs has a retro dithered-text canvas effect, and it was throwing React hydration errors. If you've never hit one: the server renders HTML, then React re-renders the same components on the client and expects its output to match the server's markup exactly. When the first client render disagrees, React throws a hydration mismatch and can throw away and repaint the subtree — flicker, warnings, sadness.

The culprit was one line reading `window.devicePixelRatio` to scale the canvas for retina screens. On the server there is no `window`, so scale defaulted to 1; on a retina client it's 2. The canvas frames generated during that first render were computed from *different scale values* on server versus client, so the two could never match — the mismatch was baked into the first paint. The fix is the standard hydration-safe pattern: keep an `isHydrated` flag that starts false and flips true inside a `useEffect`, which only fires after the component has mounted on the client. All the browser-only canvas work waits behind that flag. The server render and the first client render are now identical (both produce nothing), React hydrates cleanly, and only *then* does the browser-specific rendering — with an authoritative `devicePixelRatio` — kick in. The rule underneath it: anything that reads a browser API can't participate in the first render, full stop.

Two other tpmjs threads worth naming. First, an auth audit found sixteen cron and sync routes sharing a fail-open guard shaped like `if (CRON_SECRET && token !== CRON_SECRET) reject`. Read that carefully: if `CRON_SECRET` is ever unset, the condition is false and the route runs for anyone. A missing environment variable silently unlocks the door — and some of these doors trigger destructive cleanup and paid AI calls. They all moved to one helper that refuses to run when the secret is unset (fail-closed) and compares tokens with `crypto.timingSafeEqual`, which takes the same time whether the first byte is wrong or the last one is, so an attacker can't time their way to a valid token byte by byte. The Deno code-executors got the same treatment, with an explicit opt-out env var for local dev.

Second, the tool detail page now shows every way to call a tool — SDK, MCP, REST, CLI, and Skill — as copy-paste snippets, and makes each one actually executable from the page rather than just documented. The definitions are kept as plain data (`buildToolSurfaces` returns an array you can unit-test with no browser), so a single function feeds the docs, the tests, and the live UI without any of them drifting apart. Underneath it all, tpmjs kept moving off hosted platforms onto the box, with deploys made transactional so a half-finished one can't leave the site broken.

## tellus grew a design system with opinions

tellus — the live 3D world — got several waves of a proper design system: foundations, a dozen core components, then world-specific instruments like a compass, a minimap frame, and a live log feed, plus a ⌘K command palette wired into the running HUD so you can reach any action by typing its name instead of hunting through toolbars. What makes it more than a component dump is the accessibility discipline in every piece. The voice button carries its state through an *icon and a pulsing ring*, never color alone, because color-only state is invisible to a big slice of users. Everything respects `prefers-reduced-motion`, and every component is tokens-only and scope-prefixed so it can't leak styles into the legacy world chrome it's sitting next to. There was also a consistency pass to rip out tracked-uppercase "eyebrow" labels a parallel build had sprinkled in — back to sentence case, the kind of unglamorous work that decides whether a system feels like one hand made it.

## This blog stopped phoning home

The blog you're reading got a few upgrades aimed at being fast and self-contained. Code blocks are now highlighted with Shiki at build time. Shiki uses the same TextMate grammars and editor themes VS Code uses, but runs during the build and emits pre-colorized HTML with inline styles — so unlike a client-side highlighter that ships a JavaScript runtime and re-tokenizes every snippet on load, this ships zero highlighting JS to your browser, in dual light/dark themes off CSS variables. The fonts moved to self-hosted `@font-face` with the woff2 files vendored into the build, so the site now makes no cross-origin requests at all when a page loads — no Google Fonts handshake, no third-party anything.

The one genuinely clever bit is cache-busting the stylesheet. Browsers and Cloudflare cache `/main.css` by its URL, so if you change the CSS but keep the URL, everyone keeps getting the old file. The fix appends a SHA-256 hash of the file's contents to the link, making the URL a function of the bytes. Same CSS, same hash, same cacheable URL; changed CSS, new hash, new URL, forced refetch. It's deterministic — no manual version numbers to bump and forget — the same content-addressing idea git and every serious asset pipeline lean on. Posts also carry explicit human-versus-AI provenance labels now, which feels correct for a blog that's open about half its content being written by a language model.

Also touched: **alpha2** got a gradient-check and CPU-versus-accelerator parity harness — the kind of test that runs the same math on the reference CPU path and the accelerated path and screams when they disagree — which caught a `sum(keepdims)` bug in the CPU reference. And **toiletpaper** got its local application database made recoverable, one of those chores you're grateful for exactly once, at the worst possible moment.

A gold set, a parity harness, a hydration test, an honesty caveat about unverified word endings — most of the week was building the thing that tells you the software is wrong before a user does. The code was already good at looking finished. The measuring was what was missing.
